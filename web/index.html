Would you survive Westeros?

A survival model fitted on all 359 characters of Game of Thrones. Say who you are when you first walk on screen, and it estimates how long you last.

Spoilers for all eight seasons.

Loading the model…

“Seeing the finale” means still being alive after 64 hours of screen time: the model’s survival probability for a character introduced in the first episode. Hours are running time since your first appearance.

What the data says

Of 359 characters, 212 die. Half are dead within 29 hours of first appearing, and most of the damage is early: one in six does not see out their first hour.

What the model makes of it

Put together in one model, each trait gets a hazard ratio: how much faster or slower a group dies than the commonest group, all else equal. Left of the line is safer.

The honest summary is that these traits predict little. On characters the model has not seen, it ranks a random pair correctly about 62% of the time, where a coin gets 50. Who lives is mostly up to the writers.

The trap: “switch sides and live”

The first version of this project, written in 2020, found that characters who changed allegiance lived much longer, and built its final model on that. In the data the effect looks strong: switching appears to cut the risk of death by 53%.

It is an artefact. To switch sides you first have to stay alive long enough to do it. A character killed in their first scene is counted as loyal, and every hour a turncoat spent alive before turning is credited to the turning. Statisticians call that stretch immortal time.

To see how much the mechanism can do on its own, here are 5,000 simulated characters who die at random and switch sides at random. Switching does nothing to them.

On the simulation, counting someone as a switcher only from the moment they switch recovers the truth. On the real data that analysis is not possible: the dataset records that a character switched, not when. Restarting the clock for everyone still alive at ten hours removes part of the bias, and the effect shrinks until its interval includes “no effect”. So switching sides, and the equally circular “prominence” score, are left out of the model above.

The cast

Every character in the data. Choose one to load them into the model and mark their fate on the curve.

Allegiance is the last one the data records. That is why Tyrion Lannister is filed under Stark, and why Eddard Stark, who died as King Robert’s Hand, is under Other.

    How it was built

    The analysis is written in R and runs as one reproducible pipeline. It fits three models, scores them on characters they have not seen, and exports the chosen one as plain numbers. This page computes every prediction in your browser from those numbers; a test checks that it agrees with R to seven decimal places.

    character_data.csv
 └─ clean        pool rare groups, set reference levels
     └─ fit      Cox · elastic net · random forest
         └─ validate   20 × 5-fold cross-validation
             ├─ report   Quarto
             └─ export   model.json ──→ this page
                         fixtures   ──→ parity test (R = JS)
    Out-of-sample performance (mean of 20 repeats)

    C-index and AUC: higher is better, 0.5 is chance. Brier score: lower is better. The random forest is a benchmark; it cannot be shipped as a handful of coefficients.
